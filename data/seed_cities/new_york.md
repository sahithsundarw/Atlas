New York began as a Dutch trading post called New Amsterdam, established in 1626 at the southern tip of Manhattan. The English seized it in 1664 and renamed it for the Duke of York. For most of the colonial period it was the second city of British North America after Philadelphia, but after the Revolution its port and financial institutions grew it into the dominant American metropolis. The completion of the Erie Canal in 1825 opened the interior of the continent to trade through New York Harbor. The nineteenth and early twentieth centuries brought waves of immigration — Irish, Italian, Eastern European Jewish, and later Black Americans from the South — each leaving lasting cultural imprints on the city's neighborhoods.

Manhattan is the most densely built of the five boroughs and the one most visitors know. Below 14th Street, the street grid breaks into the irregular lanes of the original Dutch settlement, giving the West Village and Lower Manhattan a different texture from the numbered grid above. Midtown holds the corporate skyline, Times Square, and most of the Broadway theaters. Harlem, which begins around 110th Street, was the cultural capital of Black America for much of the twentieth century and remains a center of African American and Latino culture. Brooklyn, across the East River, now rivals Manhattan for restaurants and cultural activity; neighborhoods like Williamsburg, Park Slope, and Crown Heights each have distinct personalities. Queens is the most ethnically diverse urban county in the world and is essential for eating.

New York's food landscape is defined by immigration and density. A dollar slice of pizza from a streetfront shop remains one of the city's reliable pleasures. The outer boroughs hold the best ethnic cooking: Flushing in Queens for Sichuan and Taiwanese, Jackson Heights for South Asian and Ecuadorian, Sunset Park for Chinese and Mexican. The deli tradition — pastrami on rye at Katz's on the Lower East Side — is genuinely part of the city's identity, not a tourist attraction. Bagels in New York are distinctive because of the water chemistry and the boiling method; a H&H or Ess-a-Bagel product tastes different from bagels made elsewhere, and locals will tell you so.

Cultural life is concentrated enough that a week in New York barely scratches it. The Metropolitan Museum of Art requires multiple visits to cover even partially. MoMA holds the most important collection of modern art in the country. Carnegie Hall and Lincoln Center anchor classical music; small jazz clubs in the West Village and Harlem carry a living tradition. Broadway is the mainstream, but Off-Broadway and Off-Off-Broadway produce work that is often more interesting. The New York Marathon in November, the Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade, and the West Indian American Day Carnival in Brooklyn in September are the largest public events.

Getting around New York without the subway is expensive and slow; the MTA system runs 24 hours, which is unusual among world metro systems. Tap your credit or debit card directly at the turnstile — no separate card needed since 2023. Outer borough trips often require transfers between subway lines or to buses. Taxis and rideshare are practical for late-night trips or areas between subway lines. Summers are hot and humid; winters are cold with occasional heavy snow. The city is densest between 9 a.m. and 6 p.m. on weekdays; many locals do their best errands in the early morning or evening.
