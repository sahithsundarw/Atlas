Rome is among the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the Western world, a place where the layers of history are so dense that construction of a new subway line regularly halts for years while archaeologists excavate the ruins uncovered by drilling. Founded, according to tradition, in 753 BC, the city grew from a collection of hilltop settlements above the Tiber River into the capital of an empire that stretched from Scotland to Mesopotamia. The fall of that empire did not empty Rome but rather transformed it — the Catholic Church made the city its home, and for over a millennium Rome served as the spiritual capital of much of the Western world, which explains why a walk of a few hundred meters can take you from a pagan temple to a medieval basilica to a Renaissance piazza.

The city's geography is organized around its famous seven hills, though in practice visitors navigate by neighborhood. The historic center holds the Pantheon, the Campo de' Fiori market square, and the tangle of streets between the Tiber and the Colosseum. Trastevere, across the river, is one of the most atmospheric areas in the city — cobblestone alleys, ivy-covered facades, and an evening energy that draws locals and visitors alike to its bars and trattorias. Prati, just north of the Vatican, is a quieter, more residential district that offers good restaurants at slightly better prices than the tourist core. The Testaccio neighborhood, built on a literal hill of ancient amphorae shards, is considered by many locals to be the truest expression of traditional Roman food culture.

Roman cuisine is one of the most distinctive regional traditions in Italy, defined by its resourcefulness and its deep connection to specific local ingredients. Carbonara — eggs, guanciale, pecorino, and black pepper, without a drop of cream — is the most debated dish, and Romans are protective of the original. Cacio e pepe and amatriciana are the other pillars of the pasta canon, each deceptively simple and technically demanding. Supplì, deep-fried rice balls with a molten core of mozzarella, are the city's essential street snack, sold at pizzerias and markets throughout the day. Artichokes, prepared either alla giudia (deep-fried to a crisp) or alla romana (braised with herbs), are a seasonal dish that appears across restaurant menus in spring.

Rome's cultural calendar is in some sense the entire year, given that the city itself is the exhibition. The Vatican Museums and the Colosseum are among the most visited sites on the planet, but the city's lesser-known museums — the Borghese Gallery, the Capitoline Museums, the Palazzo Doria Pamphilj — hold collections that would anchor entire institutions elsewhere. Easter transforms the Vatican into a focal point for pilgrims from around the world, with outdoor mass in St. Peter's Square drawing hundreds of thousands of people. The Festa de' Noantri in Trastevere in late July is a genuinely local celebration with religious processions, music, and street food that has taken place for centuries.

Booking timed entry tickets for the Colosseum, the Borghese Gallery, and the Vatican Museums well in advance is essential — the Borghese in particular requires reservations and limits entry to two-hour slots. The city center is very walkable, and the density of sights means that significant distances can be covered on foot between major landmarks. The Roma Pass offers combined museum access and unlimited public transit for a set number of days, which can represent real savings for those planning multiple museum visits. Tap water in Rome is safe and excellent — the hundreds of public drinking fountains called nasoni are one of the city's small functional pleasures, keeping visitors cool and hydrated through the hot summer months without spending anything.
