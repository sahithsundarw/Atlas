Paris, the capital of France, has shaped Western civilization for over two millennia. Originally a Celtic settlement called Lutetia on an island in the Seine, it grew under Roman occupation before becoming the seat of the Frankish kingdom. By the medieval period it was one of Europe's largest cities, home to the first great Gothic cathedral and one of the world's earliest universities. The French Revolution remade the city's politics and its streets, and the nineteenth century brought sweeping reconstruction under Baron Haussmann, who carved the wide boulevards that define central Paris today.

The city divides into twenty arrondissements arranged in a clockwise spiral from the Île de la Cité outward. The historic core holds Notre-Dame and the Sainte-Chapelle. The first and second arrondissements contain the Louvre and the covered passages. Montmartre in the north rises on a hill above the city and retains a village character despite its tourist traffic. The Marais, straddling the third and fourth, is the oldest surviving residential neighborhood, with seventeenth-century hôtels particuliers, the Place des Vosges, and a concentrated gallery district. The Latin Quarter on the Left Bank is the student and intellectual heart of the city.

French cuisine was codified largely in Paris, and the city's food culture runs from corner boulangeries to three-Michelin-star restaurants. A proper Parisian day starts with a croissant or pain au chocolat from a neighborhood bakery. Lunch in a traditional bistro might mean steak-frites, a croque-monsieur, or a plate of charcuterie. Markets like Marché d'Aligre and Marché Bastille run on weekends and sell seasonal produce, cheese, and street food. For dinner, the narrow streets of the Marais and Saint-Germain-des-Prés fill with locals eating moules marinières, duck confit, or modern natural-wine bistro cooking.

Paris hosts a cultural calendar that runs year-round. Bastille Day on 14 July brings a military parade down the Champs-Élysées and fireworks over the Seine. Fashion Week in January and September pulls the global industry to the city for shows in the Tuileries and beyond. The Nuit Blanche in October opens museums and cultural spaces through the night. The city's museums are among the world's most visited: the Louvre holds the largest collection of art, while the Musée d'Orsay concentrates on Impressionism, and the Centre Pompidou on modern and contemporary work.

For first-time visitors, a few practical points matter. The Paris Métro runs until around 1 a.m. on weekdays and later on weekends; a carnet of ten tickets or a Navigo weekly pass covers most travel. Many smaller restaurants close between lunch and dinner service, roughly 2:30 to 7 p.m. Tipping is not expected the way it is in the United States — rounding up the bill or leaving a euro or two is sufficient. Spring and autumn are the most comfortable seasons; August empties the city of locals but fills it with tourists. Museum queues are shortest on weekday mornings.
