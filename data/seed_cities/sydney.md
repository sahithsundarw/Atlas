Sydney is Australia's oldest and largest city, founded in 1788 as a British penal colony on one of the world's most spectacular natural harbors and grown over two centuries into a sprawling, sun-drenched metropolis of over five million people. The harbor itself remains the city's defining feature — a network of inlets, headlands, and bays that gives Sydney a coastline far longer than its urban footprint suggests, dotted with beaches, ferries, and the two landmarks that have made it instantly recognizable worldwide. The Sydney Opera House and the Harbour Bridge, both engineering achievements of the 20th century, frame a central waterfront that Sydneysiders use with casual daily familiarity.

The city spreads across a large area and is best understood as a collection of distinct zones. The Central Business District and the historic Rocks neighborhood sit at the harbor's southern edge, where the earliest colonial settlement took shape. To the east, Bondi Beach is the most famous stretch of sand in the country, flanked by the clifftop Bondi to Coogee coastal walk that passes smaller beaches and rock pools along the way. The Inner West, encompassing Newtown and Surry Hills, is home to the city's creative and counterculture communities, independent bookstores, live music venues, and restaurants that punch well above their weight. The Northern Beaches stretch beyond the bridge to a series of surf beaches — Manly, Freshwater, Dee Why — that feel far removed from the urban center.

Sydney's food scene draws on its position as a Pacific Rim city with deep multicultural roots. Chinatown in Haymarket spills over into Thai Town and a dense concentration of Korean and Malaysian restaurants along Dixon and Pitt Streets. The suburb of Cabramatta is the destination for Vietnamese food, while Auburn offers Turkish and Lebanese cuisine of genuine quality. Seafood is a consistent strength: Sydney rock oysters have a distinctive briny sweetness, and a classic Sydney experience involves fish and chips eaten on a harbor wharf with resident ibis birds attempting their own share. The city's cafe culture is among the most serious in the world — flat whites were popularized here, and the standard of espresso-based coffee is exceptionally high.

Culture in Sydney balances a beach-oriented outdoor life with a surprisingly robust arts scene. The Sydney Festival in January fills theatres, parks, and public spaces with local and international performances. Vivid Sydney in late May and June projects large-scale light installations across the Opera House, bridge, and CBD buildings, drawing visitors for a three-week spectacle after the summer heat has passed. NAIDOC Week in July celebrates the cultures and histories of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, with events across the city acknowledging the Gadigal people of the Eora Nation on whose land Sydney stands.

Getting around Sydney benefits from a combination of the train network and the ferry system, with harbor ferries offering one of the most scenic commutes anywhere — the Manly Ferry in particular provides a 30-minute crossing with unrivaled harbor views for the price of a standard transit fare. The city is large and driving between distant suburbs is sometimes necessary, but parking in the CBD is expensive. Summers from December through February are hot and often humid; spring and autumn offer the most comfortable conditions for long walks and outdoor exploration. Sunscreen is not optional — the UV index is significantly higher than most visitors expect, even on cloudy days.
