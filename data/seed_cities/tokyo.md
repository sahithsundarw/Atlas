Tokyo became Japan's political center when the Tokugawa shogunate established its seat at Edo Castle in 1603. What had been a modest fishing village grew into the world's most populous city within two centuries, sustained by a rigid social hierarchy and an intricate canal network that supplied its millions of residents. After the Meiji Restoration in 1868, the city was renamed Tokyo and modernized rapidly, absorbing Western architecture and industrial methods while preserving much of its social fabric. Two catastrophes — the 1923 Great Kanto Earthquake and the firebombing of 1945 — leveled large sections, and what visitors see today is almost entirely postwar construction layered over a deep cultural continuity.

Tokyo is not one city but a collection of distinct neighborhoods that function like separate towns. Shinjuku is the commercial and transport hub, home to the world's busiest railway station and a neon-lit entertainment district. Shibuya anchors youth culture and fashion, with its famous scramble crossing and department stores stacked with clothing. Shimokitazawa a few stops west is the opposite: narrow lanes, secondhand clothing shops, live music venues, and coffee roasters. Asakusa in the east preserves the shitamachi low-city atmosphere, built around the Senso-ji temple complex and its shopping street. Yanaka, nearby, survived the war almost intact and feels like a village, with temples, craft workshops, and wooden houses.

Japanese cuisine is built on precision and seasonality, and Tokyo concentrates more Michelin-starred restaurants than any other city. Ramen here divides by regional style — tonkotsu, shoyu, miso — and the best shops draw queues before opening. Tsukiji outer market and Toyosu provide the raw material for the city's sushi culture, ranging from affordable standing bars to multi-course omakase experiences. Izakayas serve drinking food — yakitori, edamame, karaage — in a convivial atmosphere that differs from formal dining. Convenience stores (konbini) like 7-Eleven and Lawson are genuinely excellent for quick meals, with fresh onigiri, hot foods, and surprisingly good sandwiches.

The city's festival calendar follows the agricultural and Buddhist year. Cherry blossom season in late March and early April transforms parks like Shinjuku Gyoen and Ueno into outdoor parties, with families and office workers picnicking under the trees. The Sanja Matsuri in Asakusa in May is one of the city's liveliest, with portable shrines carried through the streets by thousands of participants. Summer brings Obon in August, when many Tokyoites return to their hometowns and the city quiets slightly. New Year (Shogatsu) is the most important family holiday: Meiji Shrine draws millions for the first visit of the year.

Tokyo rewards practical preparation. IC cards (Suica or Pasmo) work on trains, buses, and even in convenience stores — load one at any station. Google Maps handles Tokyo's transit network accurately, including platform numbers. Cash is still important; many smaller restaurants and izakayas do not accept cards. Shoes that slip off easily matter since many traditional restaurants and some temples require removing footwear. The city is extremely safe, including at night, and locals are generally helpful to visitors even through the language barrier.
