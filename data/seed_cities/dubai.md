Dubai transformed itself from a small pearl-diving and trading settlement on the Persian Gulf into a global metropolis within a single generation, a pace of change with almost no parallel in modern urban history. As recently as the 1960s the city's economy depended on fishing and the pearl trade; oil revenues beginning in the 1970s provided the foundation, but Dubai deliberately diversified early, building ports, free zones, and a tourism infrastructure that now draws over fifteen million visitors annually. The result is a city where futuristic skyscrapers rise directly from desert, where the world's tallest building and some of the world's largest shopping malls exist alongside historic creek-side trading neighborhoods that predate the oil era by centuries.

The city spreads along a coastal strip divided by the Dubai Creek, which historically separated the two main commercial districts of Deira and Bur Dubai. Deira retains the character of an older trading city, with dense spice souks, gold markets that operate late into the night, and abras — traditional wooden water taxis — ferrying passengers across the creek for a few dirhams. Downtown Dubai, anchored by the Burj Khalifa and Dubai Mall, represents the city's most iconic contemporary face. Dubai Marina to the southwest is a purpose-built waterfront district of residential towers, promenades, and restaurants that has grown into a fully functioning neighborhood. Al Quoz is a former industrial zone that has quietly become the city's art hub, with galleries and studios occupying converted warehouses.

The food scene in Dubai reflects its extraordinary demographic mix — roughly ninety percent of residents are expatriates, and the city's restaurants span Emirati, South Asian, Levantine, East Asian, European, and African cuisines with genuine depth in each category. Traditional Emirati food, though harder to find than imported cuisines, centers on slow-cooked lamb and rice dishes like harees and machboos, often seasoned with dried lime and warm spices. The Ravi Restaurant in Satwa, a decades-old Pakistani institution, is one of the most beloved cheap eats in the city. Friday brunch is a cultural institution of its own — a long, elaborate midday meal that occupies hotels and restaurants across the city every week.

Dubai's cultural calendar has deepened considerably in recent years. Art Dubai is one of the premier contemporary art fairs in the region, held annually in spring. The Dubai Shopping Festival in January and February draws visitors with sales, entertainment, and fireworks. The holy month of Ramadan transforms the city's rhythm, with most eating and drinking confined to indoor spaces during daylight hours, and the nightly iftar gatherings opening an opportunity for visitors to experience Emirati hospitality at communal tables.

Travelers should plan around the summer heat, which peaks between June and September with temperatures regularly exceeding 40 degrees Celsius and high humidity near the coast. The cooler months between November and April are far more comfortable for outdoor exploration. Dress codes at religious sites require covered shoulders and knees, and alcohol is served only in licensed hotel venues and certain restaurants. The Dubai Metro is clean, efficient, and air-conditioned, covering the major tourist corridors along Sheikh Zayed Road and into the Marina. Taxis and ride-hailing apps are affordable by international standards and fill the gaps the metro does not reach.
